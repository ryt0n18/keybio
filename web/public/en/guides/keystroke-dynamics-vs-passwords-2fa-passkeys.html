Keystroke biometrics vs passwords, two-factor authentication and passkeys

By Álvaro Ruiz · updated 24 November 2026 · 5 min read

Can the way you type replace your password? Short answer: no, but it can strengthen it. Keystroke dynamics has advantages no other method offers, such as working without the person doing anything different, and weaknesses that make it a poor choice as the only lock. This guide compares it with passwords, two-factor authentication and passkeys to see where each one fits.

The three kinds of factor

In security there are usually three ways to prove who you are:

  • Something you know: a password, a PIN or the answer to a question.
  • Something you have: the phone that receives a code, a USB security key or a card.
  • Something you are: your fingerprint, face, voice… or the way you type.

Two-factor authentication (2FA) combines two different kinds, so stealing one is not enough. Keystroke dynamics belongs to the third group, biometrics, and more precisely to behavioural biometrics: it does not measure a part of the body, but a habit.

The big difference: yes or no vs "looks like"

A password is either right or wrong. A passkey either signs the challenge or it does not. Keystroke dynamics, by contrast, produces a score: the way you type today looks more or less like the stored profile. There will always be some false rejections and some false acceptances, and the balance between them is set by a threshold. We explain this in the guide to FAR, FRR and EER.

This has a practical consequence: keystroke dynamics should not be the only barrier for anything important. It is, however, very useful as an extra signal that asks for one more check when something does not add up.

A password hardened by rhythm

The idea of combining the two is old. In 1990, Rick Joyce and Gopal Gupta proposed checking, as well as the password, the rhythm with which it is typed. And in 1999, Fabian Monrose, Michael Reiter and Susanne Wetzel presented "password hardening", which used typing timings to make a stolen password harder to use.

The advantage is obvious: users do nothing new, they keep typing their password. So is the limitation: a password has few characters and carries little rhythm information, so the check is less reliable than with longer text.

The unique advantage: continuous authentication

Passwords, codes and passkeys check identity once, at login. If someone then sits down at a computer with an open session, or takes over a stolen session, nothing notices. Keystroke dynamics can keep checking, in the background and as people type, that it is still the same person. That is why banks and sensitive environments use it to detect:

  • that a third party has taken over the account after login;
  • that the typist is a program rather than a person (see the guide on how to detect bots);
  • unusual behaviour, such as hesitantly typing an account number that is being dictated over the phone, a signal some anti-fraud systems use to spot impersonation scams.

The case of European banking

The EU's revised Payment Services Directive (PSD2) requires strong customer authentication, with two factors of different kinds, for many electronic payments. In its June 2019 opinion on the elements of that authentication, the European Banking Authority (EBA) listed keystroke dynamics among the "inherence" elements (something you are) that can meet the requirements, together with other behavioural biometrics. That does not mean any system qualifies, but it does show the technique has a recognised place in banking security.

What about passkeys?

Passkeys, based on the FIDO2 and WebAuthn standards, are probably the best current way to log in: there is nothing to type, they cannot be stolen by a fake site and they do not leak if the service is breached. Their arrival reduces the importance of passwords, and with them that of keystroke dynamics at login.

But passkeys do not solve everything: they protect the entry, not what happens afterwards, and they are unlocked with the device's own method (PIN, fingerprint or face). Keystroke dynamics still makes sense for continuous checking, on shared computers and as a second lock on systems that still rely on passwords.

When it makes sense

  • Yes: as an invisible second factor for sensitive access, to watch long sessions, to stop bots in forms and as a risk signal in anti-fraud systems.
  • With care: at work and in education, where you must justify that it is necessary and respect privacy (see the guide to privacy and the GDPR).
  • No: as the only method protecting something valuable, with very short texts or when people often switch keyboard or device.
