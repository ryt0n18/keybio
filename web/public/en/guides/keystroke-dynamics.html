Keystroke dynamics: what it is and how it works

By Álvaro Ruiz · updated 8 November 2026 · 6 min read

Keystroke dynamics (also called typing biometrics or keystroke biometrics) is a behavioural biometric technique that recognises people by how they type on a keyboard, not by what they type: how long they hold each key down, how long they take to move on to the next one and which letter combinations slow them down.

What is keystroke dynamics?

Everyone types with their own rhythm. Two people can type the same sentence at the same speed and still one holds the keys down slightly longer, another chains “th” or “ing” much faster, or always uses the left Shift key. These differences, a few tens of milliseconds each, are fairly stable over time and add up to a kind of typing fingerprint.

Keystroke dynamics measures those timings, builds a profile of each person from them and, when someone types new text, works out how closely it matches that profile. It is a form of behavioural biometrics: instead of a physical trait such as a fingerprint or a face, it relies on a habit.

Its big advantage is that it needs no special hardware: the keyboard people already use is enough, and it can work without the person doing anything other than typing.

From telegraph operators to keyboards

The idea is older than computers. At the end of the 19th century, psychologists William Bryan and Noble Harter studied how telegraph operators learned Morse code and noticed that operators could recognise each other by the way they sent messages, their “fist”. During the Second World War, Allied listening services used that same “fist” to keep track of individual radio operators.

Keyboards brought the first formal studies: in 1980 a RAND Corporation report (Gaines et al.) showed that seven professional typists could be told apart by the time between pairs of letters. In 1990 Joyce and Gupta proposed using it to strengthen password logins, and the technique has been researched and deployed ever since, especially in banking and security.

What is measured when you type

For each key, two moments are recorded: when it is pressed and when it is released. Almost every measure comes from those:

MeasureWhat it is
Dwell time (hold time)How long a key is held down. Usually around 70–130 ms.
Down‑down latency (DD)Time from pressing one key to pressing the next.
Flight time (up‑down, UD)Time from releasing one key to pressing the next. It is negative when the next key goes down before the previous one comes up, which is very common in fast typing.
Digraphs and trigraphs (n‑grams)How long each 2‑ or 3‑key combination takes: “th”, “he”, “ing”… Among the most personal features of all.
RhythmAverage speed, long pauses and regularity.
CapitalsWhich Shift key is used, how early it is pressed and how long it is held.
Errors and correctionsHow often Backspace is used, how quickly mistakes are noticed and how accurate the final text is.

On phones other signals can be added, such as exactly where the screen is touched or what the accelerometer and gyroscope record, but the idea is the same.

How a person is recognised

  1. Enrolment. The person types several samples (sentences, a repeated password or free text) and their profile is computed: the average and spread of each measure.
  2. Comparison. When they type again, a distance or score between the new sample and the profile is calculated.
  3. Decision. If the distance is below a threshold, the sample is accepted. The threshold sets the balance between letting an impostor in and rejecting the right person.

That scheme supports two different tasks:

  • Verification (1:1): “are you who you claim to be?” The sample is compared with a single profile, for example as a second factor at login.
  • Identification (1:N): “who are you?” The sample is compared with every profile and the closest one is chosen, or the answer is that the person is not enrolled.

There is also a distinction between fixed text (always the same sentence or password, easier to compare) and free text (any text, more convenient and useful for monitoring a whole session, but needing more keystrokes). The formulas are covered in the guide to keystroke dynamics algorithms.

Is it reliable? Accuracy and errors

No biometric system is perfect. Two kinds of error are measured:

  • False acceptance (FAR or FMR): someone else is accepted as the person.
  • False rejection (FRR or FNMR): the right person is rejected.

Moving the threshold lowers one and raises the other. The point where they meet is the equal error rate (EER), the figure usually used to compare systems. In Carnegie Mellon University’s benchmark study (Killourhy and Maxion, 2009), with 51 people typing the same password, the best of the fourteen classic methods compared, the scaled Manhattan distance, had an EER of 9.6%. With longer texts, several enrolment sessions and modern methods, error rates drop a lot.

Accuracy depends mainly on:

  • How much text there is: a few words carry little information; a paragraph carries much more.
  • When enrolment happened: enrolling on different days captures a person’s normal variation better.
  • The keyboard and the situation: a different keyboard, tiredness, cold hands or an injury change the timings.

What it is used for

  • Stronger authentication: an “invisible” second factor while typing a password or a short sentence.
  • Banking and fraud prevention: spotting that whoever is inside an account is not its owner, or that the typing comes from a script rather than a person.
  • Continuous authentication: checking throughout the session, without interruptions, that the same person is still typing.
  • Online learning and exams: platforms such as Coursera have used typing patterns to verify who was submitting coursework.
  • Research: from authorship attribution to studies of how typing changes with some neurological conditions.

Limitations

  • Variability: typing changes more than a fingerprint does. Good systems update the profile over time.
  • Imitation: studies show that, with training and software guidance, some people can mimic another person’s rhythm well enough to fool simple systems. There are also programs that generate keystrokes with fake timings.
  • Short samples: with very few keystrokes the decision is unreliable.

That is why keystroke dynamics works best as an extra layer of security, alongside a password or another factor, rather than as the only protection.

Privacy and the GDPR

The EU General Data Protection Regulation defines biometric data as data resulting from the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a person that allow or confirm their unique identification (Article 4(14)). When used to uniquely identify someone it is a special category of data (Article 9): as a rule it requires explicit consent or another exception provided by law, clear information and keeping no more than necessary. At work, data protection authorities also expect the system to be shown to be necessary and proportionate.
