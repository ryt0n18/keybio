Behavioural biometrics (also spelt behavioral biometrics) recognises people by how they do things: how they type on a keyboard, move a mouse, swipe across a screen or walk. It does not look at what you are physically, but at your habits.

What is behavioural biometrics?

Biometrics is the automatic recognition of people from their characteristics. It is usually split into two families:

  • Physical or physiological biometrics: traits of the body, such as fingerprints, the face, the iris or the veins of the hand.
  • Behavioural biometrics: learned, repeated patterns of behaviour, such as typing rhythm, the way someone signs or the way they walk.

Some techniques sit between the two. Voice, for example, depends on the anatomy of the throat but also on the way a person speaks.

The key difference is that behaviour varies more than a physical trait: it changes with tiredness, mood or the device. In exchange, it can be measured without special hardware and continuously, while the person uses their computer or phone normally.

How it works

All systems follow the same pattern:

  1. Capture: events (keys, movements, touches) are recorded with their exact timestamps.
  2. Feature extraction: measures that describe the behaviour are computed, for example the average time between “q” and “u”.
  3. Profile: several samples are used to build the person’s usual pattern.
  4. Comparison: each new sample is compared with the profile to obtain a similarity or risk score.
  5. Decision: accept, reject or ask for an extra check.

In banking and e‑commerce the decision is rarely a plain yes or no. It is usually a risk score combined with other signals: if it is high, the system asks for an extra code or blocks the transaction.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages:

  • No readers or cameras needed: it uses the keyboard, mouse or screen that are already there.
  • It can run in the background and protect the whole session, not just the login.
  • It is hard to steal: it cannot be copied like a password or photographed like a face.
  • It tells people apart from automated programs (bots).

Disadvantages:

  • It is less accurate than fingerprints or iris recognition, because behaviour varies.
  • It needs data from each person and the profile has to be updated over time.
  • It can be captured without the person knowing, which raises privacy concerns.
  • An injury, a disability or a new device can cause rejections.

What it is used for

  • Banking and payments: its most widespread use. It detects account takeovers, bot attacks and scams where a fraudster talks the victim through a transfer over the phone.
  • Companies: continuous authentication on work computers and extra checks before accessing sensitive systems.
  • E‑commerce: telling real customers from bots that buy up tickets or test stolen cards.
  • Online education: checking who is sitting an exam or submitting coursework.
  • Health (research): several studies look at whether changes in typing could help detect conditions such as Parkinson’s disease early.

Privacy and the law

In the European Union, the GDPR explicitly includes behavioural characteristics in its definition of biometric data. When used to uniquely identify a person it is special category data: as a rule it needs explicit consent or another legal basis provided by law, clear information, data minimisation and often a data protection impact assessment. The EU AI Act also treats remote biometric identification much more strictly than simple verification that someone is who they claim to be.

From a design point of view, good practice is clear: store only what is needed, process on the device whenever possible, encrypt profiles and let people delete them. KeyBio follows that approach: everything stays on the computer or in the browser.
